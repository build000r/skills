# Companion Output Structure

Every research-paper run produces four artifacts:

1. A canonical research paper
2. A companion X article
3. A companion LinkedIn article
4. A companion LinkedIn post

The paper is the source of truth. The companion outputs are derivative compression and packaging layers. They may simplify framing and pacing, but they must not introduce unsupported claims.

## Intent Boundary

- Default scope is one companion X article plus one narrow LinkedIn bundle, not a full distribution campaign.
- Use distribution principles to improve clarity, scanability, and extractability.
- Do not automatically generate channel calendars, paid-promotion plans, newsletter packs, Reddit posts, Shorts, carousels, or other multi-platform kits unless the active client overlay explicitly asks for them.

## Generation Order

1. Finish the research and canonical paper first.
2. Write the companion briefs.
3. Derive the X article from the paper and the X brief.
4. Derive the LinkedIn article from the paper and the LinkedIn brief.
5. Derive the LinkedIn post from the paper and the LinkedIn brief.

## Companion Brief

Before drafting, define one brief for X and one brief for LinkedIn:

- **Primary reader**: role, context, sophistication
- **Reader job**: "When ___, I want to ___, so I can ___"
- **Primary discovery surface**: the one surface this draft should feel native to
- **Credibility requirement**: what proof has to appear near the top
- **Share trigger hypothesis**: practical value, surprise, identity, concern, or another reason this would travel
- **Belief violated**: one sentence stating the broad assumption the opening contradicts. Aim for a belief held outside the primary reader's sub-niche — a belief in their broader category or, when honest to the paper, one held by most adults. A hook that only sub-niche readers feel is a hook with a low ceiling. Stating the violated belief here forces it to be explicit before drafting.
- **Industry bridge**: one sentence mapping the universal mechanism in the opening to the reader's specific role. The bridge lands by sentence 4 in short form (LinkedIn Post) and inside the first 150 words in long form (X Article, LinkedIn Article). Without an early bridge, a viral-shaped opening fails to convert because the reader exits thinking about the universal entity, not the offer.
- **CTA**: the one action the draft should ask for

If the client overlay supplies defaults for any of these, use them. Otherwise infer them from the topic and project context.

The belief-violated / industry-bridge fields are downstream of practitioner authority, not a substitute for it: the opening violates a belief the practitioner's authority gives them standing to overturn, and the bridge is where the practitioner's specific judgment lands. They are not a license to write contrarian bait. If you cannot defend the belief violation from the paper's evidence, weaken the violation rather than strengthen the rhetoric.

For LinkedIn, also define:

- **Professional reader fit**: role, seniority, and problem to call out early
- **Dwell strategy**: framework, checklist, teardown, case study, or another structure that rewards reading
- **Conversation target**: the kind of substantive comments/saves/sends the draft should invite

## Companion X Article

### Goal

Turn the paper into a faster, more readable piece for the X Articles composer while preserving the core argument and best evidence.

### Default Format

- Markdown or plain text by default
- 1,000-2,500 words unless the client overlay says otherwise
- One title and optional subtitle/deck
- Four to seven sections
- Shorter paragraphs than the paper
- At most one table or framework visual in the default version
- Easy to paste into [X Articles](https://x.com/compose/articles/edit) with minimal cleanup
- Headers that read like takeaways, not placeholders

### Content Rules

- Keep the same thesis as the paper.
- Choose one primary reader and one primary discovery surface from the companion brief.
- Lead with the strongest why-now angle and payoff, not the full literature review.
- State what the reader gets in the first 100-150 words.
- Add a short trust / credibility stamp near the top.
- Reuse the best numbers and citations from the paper.
- Keep one clear practical asset: framework, checklist, decision rule, or compact taxonomy.
- Prefer front-loaded headings, short paragraphs, and occasional bullets when they improve scan speed.
- Cut repetition, taxonomy sprawl, and low-signal caveats.
- End with a short synthesis, one explicit CTA, and, when relevant, a pointer to the full paper.
- Avoid Markdown features that usually need hand-fixing in a WYSIWYG editor.
- Prefer clean headings, short paragraphs, short lists, and inline links.
- Write sections so they can be excerpted into threads, newsletters, or community summaries later without extra claim inflation.

### Default X Article Structure

1. **Title**: Clear promise, less academic than the paper if needed
2. **Deck (optional)**: One sentence on who this is for and what changes after reading
3. **Opening payoff**: 2-4 paragraphs explaining the problem, why it matters now, and the thesis
4. **Why trust this**: 1-3 lines on method, experience, or source base
5. **What the evidence says**: The strongest data points and case studies
6. **What people get wrong or underestimate**: Contrarian or corrective angle
7. **Framework / checklist**: One practical model, decision rule, or taxonomy
8. **Implications**: What builders/operators should do differently
9. **Conclusion + CTA**: Tight summary plus one explicit next action

### Self-Check

Reject and rewrite the X article if:

- The opening could fit any topic with a few nouns swapped
- The first 150 words do not state a concrete payoff
- There are no concrete numbers or named examples
- There is no clear reader/job implied by the framing
- The headers do not read like skimmable takeaways
- There is no explicit CTA near the end
- The draft contains scaffolding text the user would have to delete before pasting
- The formatting depends on advanced Markdown that the composer may flatten awkwardly
- The draft drifts into a full content strategy, channel calendar, or unsupported virality advice
- The opening sentence only violates a belief held inside the reader's sub-niche, when the paper's evidence could honestly support a broader violation
- The first 150 words do not contain an industry bridge mapping the universal mechanism back to the reader's specific role
- The conclusion ends with a generic "what do you think" question instead of a single, screenshot-able takeaway line a reader could paste into their team chat

## Companion LinkedIn Article

### Goal

Turn the paper into a professional, relevance-first article that reads natively on LinkedIn while preserving the core argument and strongest evidence.

### Default Format

- Markdown or plain text by default
- 1,000-2,200 words unless the client overlay says otherwise
- One title and optional deck
- Four to seven sections
- Strong subheads that read like conclusions
- Short paragraphs and occasional bullets when they improve scan speed
- One practical asset at most: framework, checklist, decision rule, or compact taxonomy
- Easy to paste into a LinkedIn article workflow with minimal cleanup

### Content Rules

- Keep the same thesis as the paper.
- Lead with professional relevance: who this is for, what problem it addresses, and what changes after reading.
- Put proof near the top: method, named sources, case base, or concrete data.
- Optimize for dwell with structure, specificity, and patterned depth rather than hype.
- Reuse the best numbers and one strong example from the paper.
- Keep headers and opening lines explicit enough that the right reader can self-identify quickly.
- End with a question or prompt that invites substantive discussion, not generic engagement bait.
- Keep the tone professional, direct, and concrete.

### Default LinkedIn Article Structure

1. **Title**: Clear topic + outcome or contrarian claim
2. **Deck (optional)**: One sentence on who this is for and why it matters now
3. **Opening relevance block**: 2-4 short paragraphs on the problem, stakes, and thesis
4. **Why trust this**: 1-3 lines on method, source base, or direct experience
5. **What the evidence says**: strongest numbers, case studies, or comparisons
6. **What most people miss**: corrective angle or contrarian point
7. **Framework / checklist**: one practical model
8. **Implications**: what operators/builders/leaders should do differently
9. **Conclusion + CTA**: short synthesis plus one conversation-worthy prompt

### Self-Check

Reject and rewrite the LinkedIn article if:

- The first screen does not clearly identify the target professional reader
- The opening could fit any platform with a few nouns swapped
- There is no proof near the top
- The draft reads like broad motivation instead of a specific operating insight
- The structure does not reward continued reading
- The CTA is generic or bait-y
- The draft expands into a full distribution plan, calendar, or paid strategy
- The opening only violates a sub-niche belief, when the paper's evidence could honestly support a broader violation
- The industry bridge from the universal mechanism to the reader's specific role does not land inside the first 150 words
- The conclusion ends with a generic discussion prompt instead of a single, screenshot-able takeaway line a professional could send to their team

## Companion LinkedIn Post

### Goal

Turn the paper into one LinkedIn feed post that stops the right reader, delivers a concrete takeaway fast, and invites one substantive next action.

### Default Format

- Markdown or plain text by default
- Optimized for direct paste into a LinkedIn post composer
- One opening hook, short body, one CTA
- Short paragraphs / line breaks for first-screen readability
- Optional short bullet list or numbered framework
- Optional 0-5 hashtags at the end only when they improve categorization

### Content Rules

- Keep the same thesis as the paper.
- Make the role/problem fit obvious in the opening lines.
- Deliver the payoff early, then support it with one compact proof block or framework.
- Prefer plainspoken, high-signal language over cleverness or hype.
- Ask for one substantive response: a concrete opinion, tradeoff, or lived example.
- Avoid empty "agree?" prompts, engagement bait, vague productivity talk, and unsupported claims.

### Default LinkedIn Post Structure

1. **Hook (line 1)**: A sentence that violates the broadest belief the paper's evidence can honestly contradict. Aim for a belief held in the reader's broader category or, when truly defensible, one held by most adults. Sub-niche-only hooks cap the ceiling.
2. **Mechanism extension (lines 2-3)**: Name the hidden mechanism the violation reveals.
3. **Industry bridge (sentence 4)**: Map the universal mechanism onto the primary reader's specific role. Without this bridge by sentence 4, a viral-shaped opening fails to convert.
4. **Proof or framework**: strongest data point, case, or compact framework.
5. **Practical takeaway**: what the reader should do, test, or reconsider.
6. **Closer**: a single screenshot-able takeaway line a reader could paste into their team chat. Optionally followed by a specific, non-bait CTA.
7. **Hashtags (optional)**: limited, relevant, and placed at the end.

### Self-Check

Reject and rewrite the LinkedIn post if:

- The first three lines do not make the audience or payoff clear
- The post could apply to almost any topic
- There is no concrete evidence, mechanism, or example
- The line breaks feel gimmicky instead of readable
- The CTA is generic, bait-y, or asks for too much
- The hashtags dominate the draft or dilute the point
- The opening sentence only violates a belief held inside the reader's sub-niche, when the paper's evidence supports a broader violation
- The industry bridge from the universal mechanism to the reader's specific role does not land by sentence 4
- The closer is a "what do you think" question instead of a single screenshot-able takeaway line a reader could send to their team
